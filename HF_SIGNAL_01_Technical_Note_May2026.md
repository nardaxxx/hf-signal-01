# HF SIGNAL 01 — Technical Note on AI Safeguards and International Humanitarian Law

Human Flag Association
humanflag.org
May 2026

---

## Purpose

This note identifies a technical and legal distinction relevant to the 
characterization of AI safeguards in military-operational contexts.

It does not address broader questions of autonomous weapons policy or AI 
governance. Its scope is limited to one specific observation.

---

## The Distinction

AI safeguards are not a uniform category.

Some safeguards are discretionary — they reflect ethical preferences, 
reputational considerations, or policy choices that may vary by operator 
or context.

Others may implement affirmative obligations arising under the law of 
armed conflict — specifically, the requirement to recognize hors de combat 
status and to refrain from attacking persons who clearly express an 
intention to surrender.

Under Geneva Convention Common Article 3 and Additional Protocol I, 
Article 41, these obligations are binding and operational. They apply 
regardless of whether the engaging system is human-operated or autonomous.

---

## The Technical Observation

The absence of surrender-recognition capability in an autonomous or 
semi-autonomous system does not automatically produce a safer or more 
controllable system.

Under some operational conditions, a system lacking such capability 
may instead produce:

- elevated risk of unlawful engagement
- reduced operational predictability
- increased command-responsibility exposure

---

## HF SIGNAL 01 as Evidence of Technical Feasibility

HF SIGNAL 01 is a surrender recognition protocol designed to be 
interpretable by both human observers and machine vision systems.

The protocol defines machine-interpretable surrender indicators combining 
a pulsating white light signal at 7Hz for optical recognition systems and 
a continuous FM tone at 144.0MHz for structured radio signaling environments.

It was completed on April 24, 2025, formally deposited with the Legal 
Services of the Canton Ticino State Council on April 28, 2025, and 
published publicly in May 2025.

Its relevance is narrow: it demonstrates that surrender-recognition 
architecture is technically implementable. The protocol does not resolve 
all operational or legal challenges. It establishes only that the 
capability is feasible.

Where a lawful-use safeguard is technically achievable, its 
characterization as an inherent operational burden becomes less certain.
